# Design Inspiration

Find references that sharpen a design decision rather than collecting attractive images. Ground the search in the product, audience, primary task, surface, and constraints. Inspect existing product visuals and design language first when available so external references extend the product instead of replacing its identity.

## Research

- Prefer current, attributable examples from real products, first-party case studies or design systems, and credible showcases. Search broadly enough to avoid reproducing one source's composition or identity.
- Select references for relevance to the problem: information density, hierarchy, navigation, interaction, typography, color roles, imagery, motion, or another dimension that materially affects the brief.
- Inspect the actual visual when possible. Do not infer visual quality from search-result text alone, and do not present popularity or awards as proof that a pattern fits this product.
- Distinguish observed details from interpretation. Include links or identifiable source names so the user can examine the evidence.
- Treat references as inputs, not templates. Extract principles and relationships; do not copy distinctive artwork, branding, or a complete composition.

Match research depth to the decision. A narrow component question may need only a few focused examples; a new identity or major redesign may justify a broader reference set. Do not turn a small UI change into mandatory research.

## Synthesis

Organize the findings into a compact reference board or equivalent presentation that makes comparison easy. For each useful reference, communicate:

- what was observed;
- why it is relevant to the product and task;
- what principle or move could be adapted; and
- what should not be carried over, including contextual mismatch or trade-offs.

When the brief is open-ended, synthesize the evidence into two or three meaningfully different inspiration territories. Describe each territory through a visual thesis, relevant references, characteristic moves, fit, and trade-offs. The territories are decision material, not rigid style recipes.

## Handoff boundary

- If the request is research-only, deliver the synthesis and stop. Do not select a direction or modify the product without authorization.
- If the same request asks both for research and concrete direction proposals, invoke `ui-ux-baseline:visual-direction` after synthesis; it owns the proposals and selection gate. If design work may follow later but was not requested, deliver the research without expanding scope.
- If the product or user already provides a clear direction, use references only to answer unresolved questions. Do not reopen the agreed direction merely because this skill is available.
- State evidence limitations when sources, screenshots, live product access, or representative examples could not be inspected.
